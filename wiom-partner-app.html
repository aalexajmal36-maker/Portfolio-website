Case study · Product design

Wiom Partner App:
a business app for Bharat’s local internet operators

Wiom runs home internet across Tier-2/3 India through thousands of local operators. The Partner App is how they take bookings, manage net boxes, track earnings and run their team. I owned Settings, the partner’s business control panel, and worked inside the wider end-to-end V2 redesign.

My roleProduct designer · owner of Settings
TeamProduct, design & tech at Wiom
Timeline2024 – 2026
PlatformAndroid · Hindi-first, English

01 — Context

Who uses this app

A Wiom partner is a local cable or internet operator who runs a small business: they take new connections, install and recover net boxes, recharge customers on the ISP portal, and manage a few technicians.

We designed around two people the team named on the canvas: Annu bhaiya, the partner who runs the business from his phone and the WhatsApp group, and Rohit, his technician, who works from a separate app.

Most partners are more at home in Hindi than English, run on cash and trust, and judge an app by one thing: does it help me earn more today?

02 — Problem

The business lived in calls and WhatsApp

  • Money was opaque. Revenue share, bonuses, late fees and deductions landed in the wallet with little explanation, so partners called Wiom to ask “why did I get this?”
  • Net boxes went missing. Partners couldn’t easily see how many devices sat at customers’ homes, in the office or waiting to be recovered.
  • One login for the whole team. There was no clean way to add a manager or technician with the right access.
  • English-heavy, menu-heavy. Settings had grown into a long list mixing daily work with account items.

03 — Approach

Start from what is actually live

Before redesigning, I mapped the Settings journeys exactly as they existed in production, using screenshots from real phones, and set them next to what the design files said. That gave Design, Product, Tech and Ops one shared baseline.

“This section captures the end-to-end Partner App journeys exactly as they exist in production today … a shared baseline across Design, Product, Tech and Ops.”From the “What’s Live Today (SSOT)” audit board

Every gap between “designed” and “live” became either a bug for tech or a decision for product, instead of an argument in a meeting.

04 — Information architecture

Three jobs, one app

The whole app maps to the partner’s three daily jobs: grow the network (tasks and tickets), earn money (earnings and bonuses) and run the business (Settings). Settings is where everything about the business itself lives.

05 — Settings, my ownership

From a long menu to a business control panel

The old Settings was one long Hindi list of ten-plus rows. Daily-use tools sat next to logout and legal. The redesign groups by what the partner is trying to do, gives every row a one-line “what you can do here”, and moves what he checks every day (report card, wallet) closer to Home.

→

Report card: show the score, and why

Bonuses depend on two numbers, Service Strike Rate and Device Catch Rate, borrowed from cricket because partners already speak that language. The report card shows the score against the 90% target, then answers the question partners used to call about: “why don’t I get a bonus sometimes?”

Wallet: every rupee explained

The wallet shows balance, add money and send-to-bank up front, then a ledger where each line says what it was: revenue share, rating incentive, late fee, reset fee. Bank transfers show the account, fee and total before sending, and failures say why and when the money comes back.

Net box inventory: know where every box is

“Device” became net box, the word partners actually use. One screen shows every box on the account, split by where it is, with a scanner to check any box and a way to order more. If a new lot isn’t confirmed as delivered, the app blocks setup early, so the partner doesn’t get stuck at a customer’s door.

Manage team: roles, not shared logins

Three clear roles: the main number customers and Wiom call, a manager with switchable access to “Grow network” and “Earn money”, and team members who work on installs and tickets. The copy carries the weight: “make only your most trusted person manager”. We also designed for the edge case where a partner types his own number instead of his teammate’s.

Bill payment: a policy change, explained

When Wiom moved recharges to the customer’s own app, partners could no longer take cash and pay for them. Rather than silently removing the feature, the row now explains the change and gives the partner something to say: “No UPI or card? A friend, family member or shopkeeper can scan the QR and pay.”

06 — Across the app

Incentives that feel like a game

Around Settings, the wider team built a reward system partners actually look forward to: a lottery card for on-time installs, a bumper guarantee for one more connection today, and team zones where one late job pauses the lottery for everyone, so the partner chases the team, not Wiom.

Lottery, zones, booking, installation, pickup and ISP recharge flows are team work, shown here for context.

07 — What guided the work

Design principles

01

Hindi first, not translated

Copy written in the partner’s own words (net box, catch, strike rate), with English as the mirror.

02

Show the maths

Every payout and deduction is broken down, so the answer is on screen, not on a support call.

03

Block early, explain always

Stop problems before the doorstep, and every blocker says why and what to do next.

04

Design every edge

Failed transfers, wrong scans, rejected photos, own number vs teammate’s. All designed, not left to defaults.

05

Live first

Audit production before redesigning, so fixes are grounded in what partners really see.

06

Built for handoff

Shared components, Hindi/English pairs and notes for tech on the canvas.

08 — Reflection

What I’d do next

  • Test the Settings grouping with partners in two or three cities, watching where they tap first.
  • Add short Hindi audio guides to the money screens, as the installation flow already does.
  • Keep the “live vs designed” audit as a monthly habit, not a one-off.
